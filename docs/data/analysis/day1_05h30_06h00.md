## Most useful takeaways

- A thin, even throwaway prototype is appropriate when its job is to expose the real workflow. The manual work—such as calling venues—is research that should shape the automation.
- Seed selection logic with concrete examples the human already trusts. The discussion turns known venues into explicit filters instead of asking a model to invent vague criteria.
- Operational constraints often dominate product aesthetics. Permit lead times, alcohol licences, caterer/vendor arrangements, and control of the space can eliminate otherwise attractive options.
- Their knowledge-manager bot periodically asks active bots for important discoveries and extracts them into shared context. The useful pattern is continuous knowledge capture; the acknowledged danger is excessive pinging and noise.
- When the product direction changes, broadcast the change to every active worker so parallel agents do not continue implementing an obsolete plan.
- Do not rebuild commodity discovery tools. Use existing maps/search and add the missing domain-specific layer, such as capacity and event suitability.
- Treat human call notes as product data. Recording why each venue accepted or rejected the request can make the next recommendation materially better.
- Verification is not complete if the worker omits the required evidence. Here, missing screenshots/videos are treated as a process defect to fix in the reusable verification skill.

## Condensed narrative

The team keeps the event prototype intentionally small and uses real venue outreach to discover requirements. Concrete candidate venues expose capacity, permitting, licensing, and control constraints, which briefly pushes the concept from a food event toward a simpler art-exhibition format. They then update the agent fleet, emphasize reuse of existing search infrastructure, and frame human call records as a growing proprietary dataset. After a short status/contest interlude, the broadcast transitions into a product-management workshop.
