## Most useful takeaways

- A shared team bot is partly a consistency mechanism: everyone gets the same curated sources, policies, and answer style instead of training private variants that drift.
- A customer-facing technical expert needs two distinct skills: inspect the implementation accurately and translate it without exposing proprietary details or drowning the customer in internals.
- The concurrency-control example is concrete: repository workers inspect frontend/backend behavior, identify the database-level protection and hold semantics, and then produce an explanation suitable for support or sales.
- Competitive research becomes more useful when it compares the competitor's actual product journey with the home product's actual code, not marketing page against marketing page.
- Give the competitor worker a standing baseline from the codebase expert, then let the human redirect or add a research question without discarding the work already underway.
- Ask the paired workers for a decision-shaped output—here, a meaningful differentiator with low implementation effort—rather than two disconnected reports.
- The slide automation works because the output schema is rigid and the source is explicit. Its live need to resend the source is also a reminder to validate access before trusting a long-running task.
- Browser control is a useful last-mile fallback, but it is slower and more fragile than a supported API. Once the process repeats, the Day 1 advice still applies: look for a stable direct interface.

## Safety and quality caveats

- A bot with repository access should cite files/commits internally and separate verified facts from inference before its answer is sent externally.
- Automated use of competitor products must respect their terms, privacy rules, rate limits, and account policies.
- Do not let a fluent translation hide uncertainty. Customer-facing claims about security, concurrency, or data handling should have an approval path and an auditable source.

## Condensed narrative

The sales-engineering workshop moves from repeated product explanation into three live workflows. A slide bot turns trusted source material into a standardized case-study deck. A codebase expert launches cloud agents against multiple repositories and converts the result into customer language. A competitive-research bot then browses real products and consults the code specialist, demonstrating how two specialized contexts can produce a prioritized product recommendation rather than isolated reports.
