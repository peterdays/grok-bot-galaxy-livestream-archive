## Most useful takeaways

- Parallelism has a human coordination cost. One orchestrator can reduce thread switching by fanning work out and returning concise summaries, but ten agents can still overload the engineer if decisions and exceptions arrive continuously.
- Design the manager interface around attention: report decisions, failures, evidence, and state changes; do not require humans to patrol every worker thread.
- Large-codebase analysis is a promising use case because retrieval and mapping parallelize well, but the Nokia speedup is a testimonial rather than a reproducible benchmark.
- The cover-letter system demonstrates a strong architecture transferable to code: separate discovery, generation, deterministic rendering, and adversarial review instead of asking one agent to do everything.
- Give a writing critic authentic examples, a concrete rubric, and factual source material. “Sound like me” becomes testable when broken into specificity, sentence patterns, required evidence, structure, and relevance.
- Never let personalization fabricate experience. Ground generated material in a canonical resume/experience file and make truthfulness part of the critic's checks.
- Every correction is potential durable memory: ask why the error occurred, extract the smallest general rule, update the skill, and verify on future outputs. Avoid saving incident-specific clutter.
- Browser automation over people directories may violate terms or privacy expectations. Prefer authorized exports/APIs and review outreach before sending.

## Condensed narrative

This half hour moves from personal automation into two especially useful agent-design discussions. First, the speakers admit that high parallelism creates cognitive overload and suggest routing workers through an orchestrator. Then a student demonstrates a staged job-application system with separate discovery, writing, rendering, and critique roles. The closing lesson—turn every observed failure into a concise, generalized skill update—is one of the strongest reusable tips in Day 2.
