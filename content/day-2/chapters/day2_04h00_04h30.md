## Most useful takeaways

- Good prospecting is a retrieval-and-synthesis pipeline, not generic mail merge: rank accounts, identify people, find recent first-person signals, consume their long-form content, and draft against the seller's proven writing samples.
- Give the agent the final outcome. If it returns links for you to inspect, correct the delegation boundary: it should watch/read the material and produce the email, deck, or account update itself.
- Add capability gradually. Once an email workflow is reliable, extend it to another channel or a larger batch instead of specifying an enormous system up front.
- Limit noisy polling. Run inbox/account routines once or twice daily unless freshness truly matters; speak directly to a specialist when chief-of-staff orchestration adds unnecessary model calls.
- Prefer structured integrations such as an API or MCP. Browser/computer use is the compatibility fallback when a service lacks a good interface, not the default.
- A connected agent can be the retrieval layer over existing systems of record. You do not need to migrate every Slack thread, CRM field, and document into a new monolithic knowledge base first.
- A code-aware sales-engineering specialist can answer live technical questions, but customer-facing phrasing and supporting evidence still need explicit instructions and review.
- For organizational rollout, seed role-specific defaults from an experienced user's working templates, then allow individual customization.

## Condensed narrative

This sales workshop is less relevant to game implementation, but it contains a reusable agent-workflow playbook. The strongest parts show how to turn scattered customer signals into finished outreach and account updates, how to insist on completed deliverables rather than research links, and how to control cost by limiting routines and preferring direct integrations over browser automation. The last section discusses specialist boundaries, Cursor delegation, and scaling a power user's templates across a team.
