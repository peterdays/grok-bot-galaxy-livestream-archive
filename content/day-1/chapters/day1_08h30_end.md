## Most useful takeaways

- Model the real organization before automating it: an event producer coordinates venue, budget, permits, vendors, staffing, outreach, and logistics specialists. That hierarchy gives agents clearer ownership and handoff boundaries.
- Negotiation needs explicit authority limits—budget range, acceptable terms, escalation conditions, and whether the agent may send or only draft. The demonstrated safer overnight pattern is research and draft now, human review and send in the morning.
- Establish a market-informed baseline before telling an agent to negotiate. Otherwise it has no credible anchor for price or trade-offs.
- A production budget is a dependency map, not merely a spreadsheet: venue capabilities affect catering, staffing, AV, permits, and how many extra vendors must be sourced.
- Regulatory research is a good first-pass workload because an agent can gather and organize jurisdiction-specific rules. It is not a substitute for experienced legal or permitting judgment, especially where practice differs from the literal text.
- “Do it yourself as humans, then reverse engineer” is a succinct automation method: perform or map the workflow, observe hidden decisions and exceptions, and only then encode worker roles and skills.
- Long-running overnight work should favor reversible output: candidate lists, research, budgets, and draft messages—not irreversible purchases, commitments, or mass outreach.
- The candid end-of-day recap is valuable: rapid agent execution did not solve weak product focus. They built substantial infrastructure, yet still questioned whether the chosen pop-up problem was worth pursuing.
