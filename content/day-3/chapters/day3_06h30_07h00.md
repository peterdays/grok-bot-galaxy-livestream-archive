## Most useful takeaways

- Autonomy should increase by risk and evidence, not by elapsed time or prompt confidence. Begin with reversible, low-impact tasks and narrowly scoped tools; require observed success before expanding permissions.
- Persistent specialists should represent stable responsibilities, while project groups should compose those specialists temporarily. This avoids throwing away learned context at the end of every campaign.
- Treat context-portability claims skeptically until tested. Pointing a new agent at a repository may recover code context, but it does not automatically preserve conversation history, secrets policy, tool configuration, tacit decisions, or behavioral tests.
- The enterprise privacy answer is incomplete. “Opt out of training” and “talk to the team case by case” do not establish data residency, retention/deletion, subprocessors, access controls, audit logs, incident response, or regulatory terms.
- Combining multiple templates and expert sources needs provenance and licensing. Record which instructions came from each source, resolve conflicts explicitly, and do not scrape or redistribute private bot memory.
- Conversational control is only another interface to authority. A spoken “merge it” should still bind to the exact PR, verified CI/review status, protected-branch policy, and an auditable confirmation.
- Do not count accepted delegation as completion. The voice bot says it will merge one PR and fix conflicts in another, but this block does not show the resulting merge or corrected branch.
- Privacy-aware demo behavior matters: they avoid showing the operating system's share sheet because it may reveal recent contacts. Apply the same screen-safety discipline to notifications, tokens, browser history, and personal accounts.
- Any agent that can play the game can also farm rank. Separate bot and human ladders, rate-limit matches, detect automation, sign results server-side, and design incentives before leaderboards acquire value.
- PR volume is not throughput or quality. Hundreds of changes combined with a stale board and an unresolved matchmaking defect reveal coordination debt that raw merge counts hide.
- Convert repeated code-review findings into checks or skills, but target the underlying defect. A blanket “no comments” rule may suppress useful explanation as well as agent-written excuses; enforce root-cause fixes, tests, and clarity instead.
- The team's candid comparison is crucial: the broadcast prototype merges aggressively without reading code, while the production codebase receives deep review. The archive's high scores should reward this admission, not normalize the shortcut.
